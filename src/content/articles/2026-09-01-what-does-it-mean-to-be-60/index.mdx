I was at a sweet 16 birthday party recently. We all moved and grooved to the music and partied like it was 1980. You see, this wasn't just any 16th birthday — it was a leap year baby's party. In calendar years, she was turning 60, which got me thinking: what does it mean to be 60 anyway? When I was 16, 60 meant old. Now, though, it seems young.

In many countries, 67 is considered full retirement age, which is a bit startling considering that 67 is not that far off for me — I'm not even thinking about retirement yet! And it's not just me. More and more people are shunning traditional retirement assumptions and thriving. Richard Branson, well past 67, is running Virgin's businesses, still doing the things he's always done. Martha Stewart, in her 80s, is launching new ventures and striking brand deals. LeBron James is playing basketball better than many people half his age — remarkable not because he's especially old, but because in his sport, by his age, most players have long since retired. Is it time to rethink retirement age and what being old means? Should we think about 60 as the start of something new and save thoughts of retirement and getting old for later? Maybe we could call it reinvention or refinement or revitalization — perhaps even renaissance.

<PullQuote variant="display">Sixty is midlife.</PullQuote>

We're not going down the "60 is the new 40" path, because we know that's not true. We have way too much life experience and wisdom to want to be 40 again. But there is growing awareness that 60 today doesn't mean the same thing it did 20 years ago, or even 10 years ago. We know more today about how to stay healthy longer than we've ever known before. People are working longer, sometimes because they have to, but often because they *want* to and see no reason not to.

<PullQuote variant="side" side="left">For me, the party's just getting started.</PullQuote>

After all, Mick Jagger said he'd rather be dead than sing "Satisfaction" when he was in his 40s, and yet today he is arguably still the finest — and fittest — front man in rock and roll — and still singing "Satisfaction." Thinking about a young Paul McCartney dressed as a grandfather singing "When I'm Sixty-Four" seems so quaint — he's still touring the world and playing to sold-out arenas! Bob Dylan may have said it best in "My Back Pages," with his line about feeling older then, than he does now. And Keith Richards — well, he's another story altogether.

For me, the party's just getting started. I might do a few more flexibility stretches in the morning, and strength training has become a necessary habit — I still need to get my suitcase into the overhead, after all — but 60 is not a time to sit back and watch the world go by. That's for sometime in the future. Sixty is midlife. It's — as they say in Spinal Tap — the time to turn it up to 11. It's time to find my Sokéfi moments.
