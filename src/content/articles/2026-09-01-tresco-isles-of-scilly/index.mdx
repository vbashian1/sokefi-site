The Isles of Scilly, a tiny archipelago comprising around 140 islands and rocks with just five inhabited, lie approximately 28 miles off the southwest tip of Britain. Seemingly close. In reality, a world away.

It is a mythical place — of King Arthur legends, shipwrecks, and smuggling. The wild landscape contrasts with sunny beaches whose turquoise waters look as though they belong in the Caribbean, until you dip your toes in and are instantly reminded that this is the cold Atlantic after all. On stormy days those same clear waters turn angry and loud, crashing over the rocks, creating the conditions that have caught sailors off guard for centuries. The tiny graveyards scattered across the islands tell the stories of lives lost at sea, but they also tell the stories of generations of Scillonians who have called these islands home despite the sometimes harsh realities of life on a small rock in the Atlantic.

<Gallery pick={[1]} />

We have been coming here for decades. It is always the same. In a world where change is a constant, the Isles of Scilly remain stubbornly, magnificently themselves. That, more than anything else, is what makes them extraordinary.

It is a place where parents return year after year, and their children bring their children. Our family has been making the trek to the islands for four generations. And while the rest of the world changes at a seemingly faster and faster pace, Scilly's magic stays the same. It's a place that gets into your soul. You frequently overhear people swapping stories of the first time they visited the islands and how many times they've returned.

Former Prime Minister Harold Wilson — once one of Scilly's most devoted visitors — used to come here to think. Away from the weight of office, the islands gave him what they give everyone: space, silence, and the particular clarity that comes from being somewhere the rest of the world hasn't quite reached. He said he came here to get away from everything — not only in distance, but also in time. He kept coming back. When he died, he was buried at the Old Town Church on St Mary's. Today, the mod cons have arrived, but the feel and look of the islands that captured the heart of Harold Wilson and so many others remains.

Arriving in Scilly we immediately feel the stresses of the modern world start to disappear as we breathe in the fresh, salty air. The light is special — particularly in the early morning and late afternoon, when it does something extraordinary to the water. At night, with almost no light pollution and no traffic sound, the sky is dark except for the stars and the silence is as complete as anywhere in Britain.

## The Island of Tresco

Tresco, the second largest island in the archipelago, is just 2.5 miles by one mile. Like the rest of the archipelago, it is part of the Duchy of Cornwall, now under the stewardship of Prince William. Since the early 1800s it has been managed by the Dorrien-Smith family under a long lease from the Duchy. Today, Lucy and Robert Dorrien-Smith are its custodians. The arrangement gives the island a character unlike anywhere else in Britain: privately managed, carefully stewarded, and entirely car-free. Tiny e-vehicles quietly whisk guests and workers across the island, leaving visitors to enjoy the natural sounds of the birds singing and the waves crashing.

Tresco is home to one of the most extraordinary gardens in Britain, if not the world. Started by Augustus Smith in the grounds of the old Benedictine priory in the 1830s, the Abbey Garden contains an array of plants normally found only in distant parts of the world — tropical species from South Africa, the Canary Islands, New Zealand, and the Mediterranean, thriving outdoors on a British island at 50° north, because the Gulf Stream makes the impossible possible. Banana plants and palm trees grow against the backdrop of ruined abbey walls, the sound of the Atlantic audible just beyond. It is, by any measure, improbable. It is also unmissable.

You'll want your still camera here, but bring a video camera as well to better capture the sounds of the waves crashing and the vastness of the landscape.

Within the garden sits Valhalla — a collection of figureheads recovered from ships wrecked on the rocks around the islands. Every carved face, every painted figure, was pulled from a vessel that didn't make it. Today, they live on, a ghostly reminder of the power of the Atlantic and those that braved it.

In late summer and early autumn, the island downs are a haze of purple heather. In early spring, colour arrives early — the Scillies are famous for their narcissi, grown commercially and sold to the mainland, blooming months before anything appears further north, a reminder of what the Gulf Stream makes possible.

Tresco's beaches are among the finest in Britain, their emptiness a reminder of just how far the islands are from the usual vagaries of life. On most days, we pack a lunch and head out. Cromwell's Castle is the better preserved of the two — and while it is called a castle, it is diminutive by most standards. Climb the stairs inside for the views from the top. The rocky coastal path out to it hugs the beaches all the way, with views across to Bryher. A gentler cross-island option crosses the downs toward the ruins of King Charles' Castle, which is also accessible via a short steep climb from Cromwell's. Both routes are manageable for most people, but hiking poles can be useful in some of the steeper, rockier areas.

<Gallery pick={[3]} />

If you prefer bikes to feet, bikes (electric and manual) are available. The main paths are well-paved; there are grassy paths to explore beyond them. The island is small enough to cover in a day, large enough to feel like you haven't. Boaters can try their hand at sailing or kayaks. Both are available at the boat shed along with paddle boards.

When you mention you're going to the Scilly Isles you generally get one of two reactions. One — that the person has never heard of them before. The second — that they've always wanted to go. We understand both.

The islands are tiny, remote, and can be difficult to get to. But that's what makes them so special. The sometimes gruff exterior of the Scillonians hides a deep kindness and generosity — a community bound by geography, who have learned over generations what it means to depend on one another. These are people who open their homes for the night to perfect strangers stranded when a ferry breaks down. It's a place where children go to school by boat, and where the weather can quite literally cut the islands off from the rest of the world. It's a place of great natural beauty and deep history. It's a place where time stands still. It's simple. It's Scilly.

## The Essentials

**Food**
Despite Scilly's reputation as a foodie destination, there are just a few places to eat on Tresco. Dinner boats are available if you want to try the restaurants on other islands. The times and destinations will be posted on notice boards across the island. Ready made meals are available at the Island Store, or you can order ahead of your arrival to the island.

If you still have the energy after a day enjoying the fresh air and views, take advantage of self-catering units and some of the freshest ingredients you'll find. Stands scattered across the island sell fresh eggs, newly dug potatoes and lettuce, and other products on an honesty box basis. Fish and crab are pulled from the water daily. Locally made goods are also available at the Island Store.

If you're there during September, enjoy Taste of Scilly to see why the islands might be Britain's original sea-to-table and farm-to-table destination. Be sure to leave room for ice cream, or pick it up to enjoy on the beach. Troy Town ice cream is made on St Agnes from locally produced milk. Troy Town is available at the Island Store and at the New Inn.

**Where to stay**
Tresco has worked hard to ensure that its accommodations meet the needs of even the most discerning traveller. Most are airy, light-filled self-catering units located along the western and eastern coastlines. Others are more inland offering greater privacy, like Dolphin House where the Prince of Wales has been known to stay with his family.

Most accommodations are available on a weekly basis but shorter term options are available at the island's only pub, [the New Inn](https://www.tresco.co.uk/staying/the-new-inn). Those looking for a more traditional hotel might prefer the luxury [Karma Hotel](/go/booking-karma-st-martins) on nearby St Martin's. It has daily connections by boat to Tresco and the other islands. Self-catering accommodation is bookable through [The Island Office](https://www.theislandoffice.co.uk/).

## When to Go

**Summer**
Summer, with its long sunny days, remains the biggest draw, especially for families. May brings the World Pilot Gig Championships, attracting teams from around the world to race traditional six-oared pilot gigs in the waters between the islands. At the start of each race, the beaches on St Mary's fill with athletes. You can watch the races from different islands or from a boat running next to the gigs.

<Gallery pick={[0]} />

In June, elite athletes converge for the Scilly60 110, an ultra marathon across the islands, while July brings the Scilly60, a multi-day cross-island swimrun event. Both events are extraordinary given the unique terrain of the islands and they attract participants and spectators alike. We once watched the swimrun — then managed as part of ÖTILLÖ — from the top of the hill by King Charles' Castle. The athletes were well into the race at that point, having started with a run on St Mary's before swimming across to Tresco and running across the islands there. Yet even then, nearly every runner, after ascending the steep, rocky climb up from Cromwell's Castle, paused at the top to admire the view. "Brilliant view," was a common remark, before they took off again on the cross-island path.

**Spring and autumn**
Visiting in the less crowded spring and autumn can be a real delight. In the spring, flowers are in full bloom and sandy paths beckon. The islands are famous for their narcissi — daffodils that bloom months before the mainland sees them, a reminder of what the Gulf Stream makes possible. By late spring the coastal paths turn pink with sea pinks, their cushions of colour lining the cliff edges.

Come in March for the wellness. Timed to the spring equinox, Tresco's week-long Festival of Wellness offers morning yoga, spa treatments drawn from the island's own waters, cold-water immersion, and guided walks through the Abbey Garden.

September and October are some of our favourite months to visit. The days are still warm, the pace is slower, the hubbub of the summer months is over, and the water is relatively warm. September brings the Taste of Scilly food festival and the peak birdwatching season.

In April and September, low tides reveal a hidden sandbar between Tresco and Bryher — and with it, one of the more extraordinary events on the Scilly calendar. The Low Tide Event is a pop-up festival on the sandbar: local food, drinks, and music, enjoyed in the brief window before the sea returns. Times are posted on island notice boards. Don't miss it.

**Winter**
Christmas is quieter and, for those who know about it, special. Without the usual noise of high street shopping and seasonal obligation, Tresco offers bracing coastal walks, the indoor pool, a roaring fire, and the company of people who chose to be exactly where they are.

No matter when you come, don't forget to go outside on clear nights. There is essentially no light pollution in Scilly, and the skies come alive with stars. And if you're lucky — the northern lights.

## If You Have Time

**Go birdwatching**
Scilly is one of Europe's premier birdwatching destinations. Twitchers, as birdwatchers are known here, flock to the islands to see rare migratory species, sometimes blown off course as they cross the Atlantic. It's not unusual to think you're completely alone on a path and then suddenly turn a corner into a field full of birdwatchers, camera lenses and binoculars trained on a tiny bird. It's also popular with solo travellers — crowded evenings at the pub invite shared conversation about birds seen that day, and in past years.

**Puffins and seals — Eastern Isles boat tour**
The Eastern Isles are uninhabited and the wildlife is extraordinary. Boat tours run from the islands and give you the chance to see puffins and Atlantic grey seals up close in their natural habitat.

**Go off island**
Explore one or two of the other inhabited islands, or even one that's not.

<Inset side="right" pick={2} />

St Mary's — with its population of about 1,800 people, is the biggest of the group. Take an afternoon to wander through the streets of Hugh Town and out to the tiny 400-year-old Old Town Church. It sits above Old Town Beach, a tranquil reminder of the passage of time. Surrounding the church are the graves of those, including former Prime Minister Harold Wilson, who have called Scilly home.

Bryher, located directly across from Tresco, offers stunning views across to the castles on Tresco. On one end of the island is the famous Hell Bay, its thundering waves preventing people from crossing onto the farthest part of the island — leaving it for the birds to claim as their own. On the other end, a sheltered lagoon is the backdrop to peaceful strolling. This end of the island is more agricultural. Keep an eye out for stands offering freshly picked produce and small crafts for sale.

St Martin's is home to a remarkable small vineyard — self-guided tours take you through the vines to panoramic views over the Eastern Isles, before returning for an al fresco tasting of wines made entirely on site, grape to glass. They also run the UK's most south-westerly brewery, with nano-batch beers and ciders for those who prefer the grain to the grape. No booking needed — simply turn up during opening hours.

Samson, once inhabited but left wild since the early 1800s, is a paradise for those looking to get off the beaten track, even in Scilly. Take a walk on your own or join a guided walk if you prefer. You can walk around the island in a single day, picnic on its deserted beaches, and then return to what will now seem like a bustling Tresco later in the day.

**History dive**
Take a deep dive into the history of the islands. Visit the Innisidgen Hill villages on the north side of St Mary's — up country, as the locals describe it — where archaeologists have uncovered structures dating back to the Bronze Age. Before you leave the island, take a walk around Star Castle. You can enter via the steep hill that runs up from Hugh Town, or through the tiny hidden passage — watch your head, it's low — running up from behind the town. The castle, a few years — or centuries — on from the ancient villages, offers another layer of island history. As you walk around its edges you'll see the old cannons, ready for war with passing ships. Stop into the [Star Castle Hotel](https://star-castle.co.uk/) or [Tregarthen's Hotel](https://www.tregarthens-hotel.co.uk/) for a coffee break before you return to the quay.

**Deep sea fishing**
The waters around the Scillies are among the best fishing grounds in Britain. Charter boats run from St Mary's for those who want to try their luck. Whatever you catch, you know exactly how fresh it is.

**Gallery hopping**
Perhaps it's the light, perhaps it's the peaceful emptiness of the islands. Whatever it is, Scilly seems to attract artists. From watercolours to oils, jewellery to pottery, you can enjoy their works in galleries across all of the islands. [Gallery Tresco](https://www.tresco.co.uk/enjoying/gallery-tresco) — the island's own contemporary art gallery — showcases local and Cornish paintings, ceramics, and jewellery and is a good place to start.
