## The Inside Track

The moment we stepped off the plane, we knew we were somewhere new. This was a quick weekend trip for us and we were eager to get going. The arrivals hall was hot, crowded and loud — what seemed like several hundred people all trying to squeeze themselves into just a few lines. After negotiating a price at the taxi stand inside the airport, we followed our driver outside. It was warm, humid, and immediately alive. The sound of horns was impossible to miss.

Not road rage, not impatience — just the sound of the city, constant and completely normal to everyone except perhaps the people who had just arrived. Cars were jammed together in the car park, several lanes trying to become one, and we picked our way between them on foot to reach ours. On the road, a car came at us going the wrong way. Our driver went around it without so much as a raised eyebrow.

Cairo is a wonderful city where ancient meets modern — where you can be standing next to a 4,500-year-old pyramid one moment and calling for an Uber the next. We'd felt something similar in Jordan, leaving the Citadel in Amman, but this was deeper, more visceral. Perhaps it was the calls to prayer ringing out around us, perhaps it was seeing the pyramids from the rooftop of a KFC, or maybe it was the ubiquitous Coca-Cola signs along roads where donkeys were pulling carts full of grass — whatever it was, it made the city come alive in a way that more modern cities simply cannot.

That disconnect between ancient and modern was a constant throughout our time in Egypt. The enormous Grand Egyptian Museum set against the drawings on the walls at the pyramids — both telling stories, in very different ways. The lively old market at Khan el-Khalili, vendors calling out from every doorway, and the quiet of an ultra-modern shopping mall in downtown Cairo. A father teaching his young daughter to tie knots to make a carpet, exactly as it's been done for generations, while the shop owner takes electronic payment and arranges international shipping via DHL. And then there is the Nile — a river that has always held a certain mystique. In our imagination it was something out of Death on the Nile — lush palms, languorous feluccas, a river from another age; in person, it illustrated something more essential — a waterway that Cairo has been built around and depended on for thousands of years.

Together, it created an atmosphere unlike almost anywhere else in the world — one that is meant to be savoured. After all, you don't come to Cairo for what you can find elsewhere. You come for what it is.

The drive into the city centre from the airport convinced us we'd made the right decision to book tours for our visit to the pyramids. Navigating the chaotic roads ourselves would have meant losing out on the opportunity to really appreciate everything we were seeing.

## Saqqara and the Old Market

Our first stop was Saqqara, about a 45-minute drive from the centre of Cairo and home to the Step Pyramid of Djoser — the oldest pyramid in Egypt, and the oldest large stone monument in the world. We arrived early and moved through the security gates quickly. At this point you can't see much, and then you go around a bend and there it is — the step pyramid rising up in the distance. Stop for a moment and appreciate just what you're seeing — something built thousands of years ago that stands today as a reminder of the past. This is also a good spot to take a quick photo.

It's not as grand as the pyramids at Giza, but in some ways it felt more real. There's no welcome centre or gift shop. Instead, it's you and the ancient past, along with a handful of other tourists. The Saqqara site is smaller than Giza, which means you move through it on your own terms — taking in the stories told in pictures on the walls, scenes of family life, bountiful harvests, cooking, the texture of daily existence four thousand years ago. Looking at those hieroglyphics, you realise that life has a certain continuity — family celebrations, work, the rhythms that haven't changed as much as we might think. It was especially meaningful to us as we travelled with our adult children.

There was a sandstorm on the day we were there. It meant we missed the sharp contrast of the sand-coloured pyramid against a clear blue sky, but it also meant we had the site largely to ourselves. Even with the sand blowing and biting at our skin at times, one of our favourite parts of Saqqara was its feeling of remoteness. From the ruins, you could see the Sahara Desert spanning out for miles and miles. While we could only imagine how it would look on a clear day, we thought the sandstorm gave it a special feel.

We took shelter from the blowing sand by going inside — a short walk down a narrow, sloping ramp into a dark room where a guide waits to illuminate the secrets of the pyramid: walls covered in hieroglyphics, rows of tiny, precise detail.

You can go inside the pyramids at Giza too, but the passage there is steeper and longer, and the walls of the interior room are bare rather than covered in the fine etchings of a past civilisation. Saqqara offers something more intimate, and for us, more unexpected.

That evening we went to Khan el-Khalili. Our Uber dropped us at a busy corner near a mosque, where the sidewalks were filled with goods for sale. During Ramadan, the market after dark has a celebratory atmosphere — everyone out, calls to prayer drifting from the large mosque nearby, groups heading off to prayer and returning, the whole place moving with a collective energy that's particular to this time of year.

<Gallery pick={[1]} />

The market sells everything from souvenirs to spices, jewellery to leather goods — it's busy, noisy, everything you'd probably expect from one of the oldest markets in Cairo. There, and across the city, you'll find opportunities to buy cartouches — personalised hieroglyphic name plates made of gold or silver. Cartouches are meant to protect against evil spirits both in this life and in the afterlife. As you wander through the market, whether you intend to buy or not, you'll almost certainly be approached by sellers. Our evening talking with them was memorable and so kefi.

We bought leather slippers from a man who was completely certain we were French. One of us speaks French, which settled it as far as he was concerned, and the negotiation proceeded accordingly — in French, with an Arabic accent, entirely on his terms. To demonstrate the leather was genuine, he ran a lighter across it. Real leather doesn't burn, apparently. We hadn't known this, and found the fact that he felt compelled to prove it at all more interesting than the demonstration itself. He then mentioned, with great confidence, that French President Emmanuel Macron had been in the previous week and bought the same pair. We gave him full marks and bought the shoes.

## Giza and the Grand Egyptian Museum

We drove past the pyramids the afternoon before we visited them, and saw nothing. By then the sandstorm had thickened — heavier than it had been at Saqqara that morning — and the most famous monuments in the world were simply not there, erased by the weather. The next morning was clear and still. Standing where the pyramids had been invisible the day before, it seemed genuinely impossible.

The welcome centre is new and efficiently organised, with the full range of camel rides and souvenir opportunities. We moved through it quickly. The pyramids themselves don't need any framing — standing next to them, you understand something about scale that photographs cannot convey. The limestone blocks that form them are enormous, and you find yourself marvelling at the engineering — millions of tonnes of stone, quarried, transported, and set in place without any of the machinery we would consider essential today. We walked all the way around all of them. They are bigger than you think they are, even when you think you've accounted for how big they are.

We didn't go inside — the internal passage is steeper, longer and narrower than Saqqara, and we decided against it. After the majesty of the pyramids, the Sphinx was smaller than expected. It sits down the hill in front of the pyramids, guarding them today as it has for more than 4,000 years — and it sits directly across from the city of Cairo. The proximity of the pyramids to the city is surprising, another instance of the ancient meets modern that defines Egypt. While Saqqara feels remote, the desert at its base stretching away seemingly without end, at Giza the busy streets of Cairo are almost within spitting distance. Ancient and modern, literally side by side.

The Grand Egyptian Museum is about a mile from the Giza complex and a natural pairing for the same day. It's vast. If you want to see everything, spending just one minute looking at each of the 100,000 artifacts on display would take almost 70 sleepless days. We recommend having a plan, using a guide, or both. The Tutankhamun collection draws significant crowds; the rest of the museum, by contrast, is relatively uncrowded.

Book your tickets in advance. The museum's booking site runs on the Egyptian government's payment system, and it's not uncommon for foreign cards to be rejected. Booking your visit through GetYourGuide avoids this issue and also includes a skip-the-line feature.

For us, the Tut exhibit was a priority. We'd seen it before when it was a travelling exhibit, but seeing it here was something else entirely. We'd stood next to the pyramids, learned about the process of preparing the pharaohs for the afterlife, understood something of the scale and belief system that produced all of this — and now here was one of the most famous pharaohs in history, perhaps the most famous, in close-up. The context made it real in a way the travelling exhibit never quite managed.

Elsewhere in the museum we followed the story of the archaeologists themselves — the challenges, the discoveries, the ongoing work of uncovering what's still buried. Having been at Saqqara the previous day, seeing the excavations still underway, we found ourselves wondering what new stories are still waiting to be told.

## The Essentials

**Getting around**
As a rule, we like to self-drive — but Cairo is one of those places where we decided to use local transportation options. The roads can be chaotic, often with more than one car per lane, and road signs are minimal. Ubers are easy to get and reasonably priced.

Even in the back of an Uber, getting around is an adventure in itself. The streets can be crowded, especially at rush hour. It's not uncommon to see people crossing multiple lanes of traffic on foot, weaving between cars. Fender benders are a regular occurrence — we saw several during our short visit, including one car that had somehow managed to end up straddling a raised median. As we drove past, a small group of people were attempting to lift it off.

As the city thins out towards its edges it becomes more agricultural — stray dogs a regular presence, the dense urban fabric giving way to something older and slower. Throughout the city, and especially on the outskirts, you'll find koshks — the traditional tiny neighbourhood shops carrying an array of goods. Even here, reminders of the juxtaposition of modern day and old Cairo are present. You'll see it as you walk past the Coca-Cola fridge and along the aisles displaying traditional Egyptian foods.

**Food**
Cairo's food scene is one of the great surprises of the city — rich and varied. Like much of Cairo, it comes alive in the evening.

We were there during Ramadan, which meant the city operated on a different rhythm — quiet during the day, then suddenly, completely alive after iftar. Nowhere was that more apparent than at Khan el-Khalili in the evening, where the cooking smells hit you before anything else — falafel, shawarma, charcoal-grilled kufta, sugar and pastry from the sweet vendors. A city that had been fasting all day and was now, collectively, eating.

We sampled as we went — falafel and shawarma both from street stalls and sit-down spots, kufta, and an array of sweets that Egypt does particularly well: baklava, and konafa — the shredded pastry filled with cheese or cream and soaked in syrup, scattered with pistachios, particularly associated with Ramadan when it's made fresh everywhere. Nuts in general are a constant presence, sold from carts and small shops throughout the market. One of the things we always do in a new city is look at the grocery stores — the displays tell you more about a culture's daily food life than any restaurant guide. Cairo's are worth an hour of your time.

Felfela — a long-standing Cairo institution — is where to find Egyptian dishes rarely made outside the home kitchen. We tried the molokhiya and the kufta. And wherever you are in the city, keep an eye out for the small coffee houses that seem to appear on every corner — thick Turkish coffee, cardamom-spiced, served in a copper pot with a long handle.

**Where to Stay**
We stayed at [The Nile Ritz-Carlton, Cairo](/go/marriott-nile-ritz-carlton). The hotel itself opened in 1959 as the Nile Hilton before becoming a Ritz-Carlton in 2015. Like so much of Cairo, this hotel also has a story to tell, albeit a more modern one. Over the years it's been a destination for numerous celebrities including Frank Sinatra and Elizabeth Taylor. The hotel is well located, the service was warm and professional, and between the Club Lounge and several on-site restaurants including Vivo — consistently rated among Cairo's best — there was no shortage of options if we wanted to enjoy a meal without leaving the hotel.

If you'd like to wake up to a view of the pyramids, consider the [Marriott Mena House](/go/marriott-mena-house). Its gardens back directly onto the Giza plateau — the pyramids are less than half a kilometre away.

**Tipping and cash**
There is a tipping culture in Egypt — guides, drivers, the man who illuminates the hieroglyphics in the pyramid. Having small bills on hand makes this straightforward. Cards are increasingly accepted but cash remains essential for markets, smaller sites, and tips.

**Local laws**
Alcohol is available in licensed restaurants, bars, and hotels — not freely available elsewhere. Drugs are illegal with zero tolerance. Drones require explicit permission from the Egyptian Ministry of Defence.

## If You Have Time

**Coptic Cairo**
Take a break from the vibrancy and chaos that makes Cairo so intriguing and visit Coptic Cairo. From our hotel we drove along the Nile Corniche road where we could see the same boats and cafes we'd seen from our balcony. Coptic Cairo itself is almost completely car-free, making it a welcome respite from the honking horns and general busyness that are everywhere else in the city. As well as being a UNESCO neighbourhood in old Cairo, Coptic Cairo is an important religious centre — one of the rare places in the world where Christian churches, synagogues and mosques stand close together.

**Saqqara's Carpet School**
On the way back from Saqqara, we stopped at a carpet school nearby — a place where children learn the craft, the finished carpets then sold to visitors. It raises questions that don't have clean answers: child labour is a reasonable concern, and so is the fact that this is skilled work that matters economically to the families involved. We watched a father and daughter working together on the same carpet and learned to tie the knots ourselves.

**Mastabas of Qar and Idu**
We spent most of our time at Giza exploring the pyramids and the Sphinx, but had enough time for a quick detour to the mastabas of Qar and Idu — noble officials' tombs in the Eastern Cemetery just east of the Great Pyramid. These flat-roofed rectangular tombs, built around 2300 BC, contain extraordinary carved wall reliefs depicting daily life: cattle being driven across marshes, people dancing, food being prepared, scenes of a world four thousand years gone but somehow still legible. A false door — the symbolic threshold between the living and the dead — still shows traces of its original red paint.

**Nile Taxi**
Take one of the Nile Taxis — motorised water taxis that run along the river — for a ride through the city. These can be a great time saver during rush hour, and regardless of when you take one, you get a slower-paced view of old Cairo from the water. For a more traditional experience, feluccas — the classic wooden sailing boats that have plied the Nile for centuries — are also available and bookable through GetYourGuide or your hotel.

**The Old Egyptian Museum**
The original Egyptian Museum on Tahrir Square — open since 1902 — is still operating, though it's in transition. Many of its most famous pieces, including the bulk of the Tutankhamun collection, have now moved to the GEM.
