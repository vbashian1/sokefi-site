Walking the higgledy-piggledy streets of Shrewsbury, a lively market town in Shropshire in England, it's easy to imagine what it was like in the days of one of its most famous residents, Charles Darwin. Shrewsbury is located north west of London, within easy reach of Birmingham and Manchester. It's home to the Shrewsbury School, where Darwin was a student. Today, the school stands above the town, looking out across the Severn river. When Darwin was a student, its buildings were more centrally located. You can still visit the library located in the centre of town where Darwin once sat, and it's here where our tour begins.

As you head into the library, stop for a moment to look at the statue outside of Darwin as an older man. Then, head inside and to the Darwin room. There, you can imagine a young Charles Darwin completing his school work, possibly even beginning the thought process that eventually led to his groundbreaking theory of evolution. The building has, of course, been renovated since Darwin's time, but it still retains many of its old structural elements. Note for example the old stone work and the timber framing. As you walk through the building, keep an eye out for names carved into the windowsills. You won't find Charles Darwin, he wasn't a fan of the school, but you will see the names of other students who once sat in the classrooms there.

Back outside, wander through the chaotic jumble of streets in the town centre. The town retains much of its historical character from Darwin's day and even earlier, making it easy to imagine how it might have looked when Darwin walked through the streets, first as a boy, and then as a young man. The black and white timbered buildings stand in stark contrast to some of the more modern architecture that has snuck in over the years. Today, the usual hallmarks of the 21st century, McDonald's, Starbucks, and Greggs, the popular British fast food chain, stand as reminders of the passage of time.

Continue on to the old market hall, which is sometimes referred to as the new market hall despite the fact that it was built in 1596. It did, though, replace an earlier market hall on the same site, built in the 1560s — traders had gathered on this spot since the 1260s, but that earlier hall building itself came later. New, or old, the existing building was constructed to house market stalls on the ground level and an assembly room on the first floor. Today, it's home to a cinema and a small café. If you have time, stop in for a break and look out at the square which in many ways looks similar to how it looked in Darwin's days.

Next, continue to The Mount, otherwise known as Charles Darwin's boyhood home. On your way, make a detour past the Dingle. Today, the Dingle is a stunning, beautifully landscaped oasis filled with flowers and fountains. Its award-winning gardens attract horticulturalists and gardening enthusiasts from around the country. During Darwin's youth though, the Dingle was still known as the Wet Quarry — a swampy, marshy area not yet transformed into the gardens you see today, though it's easy to imagine as you stroll through the paths. Notice how the elevation changes as you move through the gardens. The swampy marsh that existed in Darwin's days, though, was the perfect breeding ground — no pun intended — to capture the attention of a young Darwin, and plant the seeds of his later theories. Its location, roughly between his school and his home, meant that Darwin had easy access to the riches it offered. Darwin was a boarder at the Shrewsbury School; however, he often escaped its confines to spend time in his home, likely stopping at the Dingle on his way.

Continuing on to The Mount, the name of the road where Darwin's home stands, you'll cross the River Severn, the long, lazy river that cuts through the town. There are several bridges that cross over the river and paths alongside. Spend a few minutes wandering along. At most times of the year, you'll see racing shells filled with rowers practising for races. The students are often associated with Darwin's alma mater, now located up on the hill overlooking the river.

When you arrive at Darwin's birthplace and boyhood home, his comfortable upbringing is evident. The house itself is large and it sits on 7 acres. Various outbuildings sit behind the house including a greenhouse. Darwin often claimed that he was born a naturalist, and seeing the place where he grew up, it's not hard to understand his claim. The Mount is only open at certain times of the year, such as during the Darwin Festival, which takes place in February. Advance bookings are required and tickets are limited. Check the website to see if it'll be open when you're there. If it's not, enjoy the exterior, noting its grand facade and the position of the house above the River Severn.

After completing his education at the Shrewsbury School, Darwin left Shrewsbury to continue his studies, first at Edinburgh University in Scotland, and later at Cambridge. He then returned to Shrewsbury to embark on what would be a remarkable and transformational journey.

As you retrace your steps from The Mount back across the Severn and the Quarry, and through the streets of Shrewsbury, imagine the excitement he must have felt as he began his travels. Today, your destination is the Lion Hotel on Wyle Cop, just over a mile from his boyhood home. Heading down the hill on St Mary's from the centre of town and then along Dogpole, you'll see the Lion Hotel. It was here that Darwin boarded the stagecoach that would take him to the HMS Beagle, bound for the Galápagos Islands.

At the time, Darwin couldn't have known what lay ahead, both in terms of what he would discover nor the treasures he would bring back. The reason for his journey was to fulfil his role as a naturalist on the HMS Beagle, where his responsibilities included helping to map the coastline of South America. The Galápagos Islands were simply the planned stopover for the ship. The journey was a long one — taking about 3 years and 9 months before it arrived in the Galápagos, where it would dock for about 5 weeks. Darwin used that time, moving from island to island, to collect specimens of plants and fauna, and to take copious notes of the wildlife he observed.

At the end of the 5 week period, Darwin reboarded the HMS Beagle to continue his journey, eventually returning to England. The return journey was shorter, taking just about a year. Darwin would never return to the Galápagos or even take another journey by ship. Instead, he conducted his research in England. Today, many of the specimens Darwin collected are in the [Natural History Museum in London](/go/nhm). Darwin briefly returned to Shrewsbury following his journey on the HMS Beagle, before making his permanent home in London and later Kent. He did, however, continue to visit his boyhood home for many years.
