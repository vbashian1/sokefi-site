As a lifelong solo traveller — I was doing it before it even had a name — I get a lot of questions about the ins and outs of solo travel. So, I thought I'd share some of my insights about solo travel — how to do it, and what it means to be a modern nomad.

One of the questions that is most frequently asked is whether I ever get lonely on my solo adventures — and the honest answer is, sometimes. But far less frequently than you might think. There's always someone to talk with, and I find that striking up conversations with locals and other solo travellers can be fun. Hotels with common spaces also offer a place to connect with others and share experiences. Another question that comes up regularly is about how long a solo trip should be. The answer is the same as you'd give for any other type of trip — it depends on your schedule and your budget. My advice to a new solo traveller would be to keep it short initially — a few days away — maybe even just a single night — to get a sense of what solo travel means to you. I think you'll quickly sense whether you prefer to spend your days entirely alone — choosing your agenda entirely on your own — or whether you prefer to join a group tour for a few hours and share the experience of a new place.

Which brings me to another question that comes up regularly from friends and from people I meet on my travels. What exactly is solo travel? Is it still solo travel if you join a group? If you join a group of solo travellers for a hiking trip in the Dolomites, is it still solo travel? How about if you just participate in a half-day walking tour through Paris? Does a week-long cruise to Alaska during which you participate in shore excursions count? My answer to all of these would be yes!

I'm not a purist when it comes to solo travel and I certainly don't think it means that you have to go off into the wilderness and be completely alone for several days. My definition of solo travel involves making the decision to head out for an adventure whether it's for a single night or for three months. It doesn't matter whether you spend part of that time with strangers who may end up becoming lifelong friends. Nor does it matter if you start off with a friend, partner, or spouse but then continue a trip on your own for a few extra days or add a few solo days on the front. That's why I like to use the term modern nomad instead of solo traveller. To me, solo travel involves making your own decisions.

One of the most common questions I get from other women, but less frequently from men, is where I eat when I'm travelling on my own. What I've come to realise is that the question isn't really about the location as much as how I go about eating alone. In fact, that question was the inspiration for Reservation for One — it's solo travel at the most basic level. And I get it — there's a societal norm of always eating in restaurants as couples or in groups, but as more women reach the upper echelons of the workforce and more women make the decision to live independent lives, that norm seems to be rapidly changing, and for the better. We're modern nomads! It's common now to see solo women having dinner at the bar in a lively restaurant — one of my favourite ways of dining in the evening when I'm travelling on my own. The bartender will usually stop by for a chat, can be a great source of information on the local area, and because you're sitting at the bar, the discomfort you might feel sitting alone at a table disappears.

If you're not ready for that, try having a bigger midday meal when there are more single diners around. One of my favourite things to do on trips, especially trips to new cities, is people-watch. Sitting outside at a café or inside at a window and watching the world go by is one of the great joys of travel. Sometimes I take a book or a notebook along, other times, I don't.

If you're a solo traveller within a guided group tour, you'll almost certainly come across other travellers, solo or otherwise, who'd love to swap stories over meals. Sometimes, if it's been an especially busy day, I just go back to my hotel for room service or a takeaway meal eaten in the club room. The one great thing about meals as a solo traveller is that you get to choose what to eat and when.

Safety is a very real concern for people, especially women, as they consider solo travel. How do I stay safe when travelling on my own? My advice here is to take the usual precautions you'd take in daily life. I also keep an emergency credit card separate from the one I carry, along with copies of my passport and other travel documents. Have someone who knows your plans — even if they're loose — that you check in with on a regular basis — via social media, email, phone — whatever works for you. It can be reassuring to know that someone, somewhere, is there for you.

Knowing you're not alone when trips get disrupted also offers peace of mind. I always travel with insurance — not because I expect disaster, but because things happen — flights get cancelled, connections get missed, bookings fail — you name it. Disruptions like these can be stressful, and having insurance helps reduce the stress.
