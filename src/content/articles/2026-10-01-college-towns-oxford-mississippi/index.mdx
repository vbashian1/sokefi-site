There's something irresistible about visiting college towns, in the autumn especially, but they're great places to visit in any season. They tend to be lively places at any time of the day and well into the evening, there's always something to do — musical performances, art shows, plays — you name it. There's generally a brewery or three or four — all serving a range of inexpensive foods. Unless it's exam week — and in some cases even when it is — there's an exciting game or match to watch. Architecturally, college towns are often a delight — from the campus to the surrounding communities. What I think I like best about visiting college towns though is that intangible element of people coming together — often for a short time — to learn and experience new things. It brings a sense of optimism and curiosity that shapes the entire town.

I've been to college towns all over the United States — to me, they're a perfect getaway for solo travellers. The very nature of a college town means that you never feel alone — people are always striking up conversations with people they don't know. The towns are usually small and easy to navigate and I find that I usually have more activities that I want to do than I have time for. In fact, the biggest challenge isn't finding enough to do, but figuring out what not to do — that and choosing which college town to go to in the first place.

If I had to choose one, Oxford, Mississippi would definitely make the short list of quintessential college towns. You feel it the moment you drive into town. The particular shades of red and blue that scream Ole Miss are everywhere — in shop windows and on signs in front of the businesses that line the roads leading into the university. If you don't support the Rebels — you might as well turn around and go back to where you came from. The mascot, Tony the Landshark, replaced an earlier one — but the tradition and connections are not new.

Heading up the drive into the university, you go past the signpost announcing where you are. Ole Miss — a university that has been the star of Mississippi for almost two centuries. This is a school that attracts generational students. It's not uncommon to talk to students whose parents and grandparents were Ole Miss students. The campus is just what you'd expect — beautiful brick buildings, a collection of buildings with Greek letters — sororities and fraternities are huge here — and the expansive lawns and tall trees that come together in that perfect harmony that makes the university look as though it came out of central casting.

One of my favourite parts of Oxford is the town square that sits adjacent to the university. It's a large square with independent shops and restaurants on all four sides and a huge courthouse in the middle. Here too, you'll find windows emblazoned with Ole Miss red and blue — the displays carefully curated to show the connection to the university. You can easily spend a day browsing through the Square, wandering in and out of the many shops, stopping for a coffee or lunch along the way. One of my favourite places to stop for a while is [Square Books](/go/square-books) — a local independent bookshop. There are actually several Square Books dotted around the Square — each with a specific focus — children's books, cooking and lifestyle books, and so on. I usually make a beeline for the main location. It's filled with books from local authors as well as a wonderful selection of authors from around the world. I usually stop at the local authors displays first.

One of Oxford's most famous authors is John Grisham and of course, his works are well represented — but there's something about standing in the shop across the Square from where he did some of his early writing that makes it all so very real. If you're a John Grisham fan, you can walk past the building — an old church — it's just off the Square — and picture him looking out, perhaps seeing the top of the courthouse — and writing the legal fiction that has captured the imagination of readers across the world.

Oxford was also home to William Faulkner — who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1949. A visit to Faulkner's house — [Rowan Oak](/go/rowan-oak) — gives insight into what made him tick. It's located near the university — down the road from the Square. The grounds are big — filled with leafy trees and big lawns. It has that feel of the Old South — of an antebellum mansion where time has stood still. Faulkner bought it in 1930 and lived there until his death in 1962. When you're on the Square, you'll notice Faulkner Alley, a skinny passageway that leads to the drug store where Faulkner often borrowed books.

Further on the Square and around the corner is [Neilson's](/go/neilsons-oxford) department store — the oldest department store in the South. It's on the historical register, but you'd know it's special just by the entry way. Established in 1839, the building is a study in attention to detail — from the gold trim on the exterior walls to the beautiful wood doors that lead into a quiet oasis of neatly displayed classic clothing and accessories. It's shopping the way it used to be.

Before you leave the Square, be sure to stop and read the sign giving a short history of Oxford and the names of some of its most famous residents. You'll find it at the opposite end of the same side of the street as Neilson's.

## If You Go

**Getting there**
Oxford doesn't have a major airport of its own; the easy approach is to fly into Memphis and drive down — it's about an hour and a half. Memphis's music sights (or a drive down the [Blues Highway](/articles/blues-highway-highway-61/)) make an easy add-on on the way in or out.
