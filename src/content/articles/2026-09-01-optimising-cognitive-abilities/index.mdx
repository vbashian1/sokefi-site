A new study by researchers at the Center for BrainHealth at the University of Texas at Dallas suggests that what has long been accepted within the medical community — that we lose cognitive abilities as we age — might not be quite right. As it turns out, the new study shows that even people in their 70s and 80s can improve their problem-solving abilities, focus, and their ability to reason. What's the secret?

To be sure, our brains do lose mass with age, but the news that loss of brain mass doesn't have to mean loss of cognitive abilities is a welcome reversal of what had been believed to be a normal part of ageing. According to Dr. Lori Cook, director of clinical research at the Center for BrainHealth, "For far too long, the medical community and the general public have operated under a 'decline-based' model of ageing – the outdated misconception that our cognitive peak happens in our 20s or 30s, followed by an inevitable downward trajectory." She says that instead of linking the brain to age, we should instead focus on possibilities.

Think you're already firing well on all cylinders so you don't need to worry about brain health, at least right now? Maybe. But the research shows that everyone can benefit from taking steps to improve our cognitive abilities. In fact, the best time to start, regardless of where you are, is probably right now. You don't need to wait until you get older, whatever that might mean for you. The new study is just one of a growing number of studies all pointing to the same conclusion, we shouldn't automatically assume that we will experience cognitive decline as we age.

More research is needed. One question raised by the research for example is if we do lots of crossword puzzles or similar activities, do the brain benefits spill over into other areas of our lives or do we just get really good at crossword puzzles.

So, what should you do now? Well, keep in mind that we are not doctors. Our job here is to raise awareness of studies like these. You should of course, talk with your medical providers about what this means specifically for you. In general, though, the study suggests that we should consider actively working to improve our cognitive skills by incorporating daily brain breaks, during which we disconnect from everything and just let our brain recharge, deliberately try to solve complex problems by thinking outside the box, turning instead to creative and innovative solutions, and dedicate focused, uninterrupted time to one or two high energy goals every day. Creating energy-based to-do lists every day instead of standard checklists can also help boost brain power, as can working to limit information overload. Dr. Cook claims that "If people can dedicate just 5–15 minutes a day to adopting intentional, brain-healthy habits, they may be able to shift their cognitive trajectory."
