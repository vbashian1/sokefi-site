Have you thought about a long weekend in Iceland? In the winter? We did.

It was a last minute trip. We'd been planning to go somewhere else entirely but couldn't pass up a cut rate fare to go to a place that had long been on our bucket list. The catch? We were going to Iceland for just under 48 hours.

As we began our descent over Reykjavík, we could see glowing red embers, a sharp contrast to the darkness. A volcano had erupted a couple of days earlier near the airport, giving us an unexpected navigational tool.

We arrived in almost complete darkness, something that's not hard to do in Iceland in late December. At that time of the year, there are just four hours of sunlight. For us, that meant two things — first that we'd probably see few other tourists during our visit, and second, that we would need to be intentional with our time.

That night we stayed at the [Courtyard by Marriott](/go/marriott-courtyard-keflavik) near the airport. We were picking up a car from Europcar the next morning and wanted to be close by.

Rising early the next morning, we had breakfast at the hotel — one of those wonderful Scandinavian inspired meals. Outside, it was still very dark, the only light coming from street lights. Inside, we sat in a lively breakfast room, overhearing the plans of other travellers getting ready to leave for the day.

Our own plan involved watching the sun rise over Reynisfjara before going on to the waterfalls. It was still dark when we left, but our route meant that we'd be passing back through the same area we were setting out from later in the day — when it would be light — so we figured we wouldn't miss anything now.

<PullQuote variant="display">We were in Iceland. In December. On the Winter Solstice. It was dark.</PullQuote>

Driving out past the burning embers we'd seen from the plane the night before, we were grateful for the coffee we'd had. We weren't jet lagged — we'd just flown in from London, a relatively short 3-hour flight. But we were in Iceland. In December. On the Winter Solstice. It was dark.

<Inset side="right" pick={0} />

Thanks to Google Maps, we arrived at Reynisfjara — Iceland's famous Black Beach — right on schedule just as the faintest light was beginning to show in the sky. We sat on the cold black sand and watched as the new day began. In Iceland in December, it's not like sunrise in the summer or even a December sunrise in many other countries. Instead, daylight is often a watery muted light that disappears again almost before you've had a chance to appreciate it.

We'd brought both a video camera and a still camera with us — we knew we wanted the video camera for the sunrise and waterfalls, but wanted the still along as well. Watching the sunrise, we were glad we had both.

Now that it was light, it was time to see the waterfalls that Iceland is so famous for. First though, a stop for food. It had been many hours since breakfast and we had a full day ahead. We'd seen a promising option on our way to the beach and it did not disappoint. The Vík Icewear Mall is a terrific mix of traditional Icelandic wool sweaters, restaurants, and a grocery store — all under one roof. You can even learn a little about how the wool used in the sweaters is spun. The bakery looked inviting, but for us though, it was a visit to the grocery store where we bought unfamiliar car snacks and other provisions to set us up for the rest of that day. This was a quick trip for us and as inviting as the restaurant looked, we wanted to continue with our plan.

Our next stop was Skógafoss. As we stood at the base of the massive curtain of water in front of us, we knew we needed to hike to the top. The trail up was steep and icy. Just what you'd expect in Iceland. It had stairs at spots, and we noticed some people wearing crampons. We didn't have them but were glad of our lugged sole boots. As we climbed, the sound of the water crashing below thundered around us. At the top, you could see far into the distance.

<Inset side="left" pick={1} />

From there we drove on to Seljalandsfoss. Despite being just a few kilometres from Skógafoss, Seljalandsfoss is an entirely different experience. The falling water seemed gentler, almost misty. We walked along the path that goes behind the falls where the mist seemed to envelope us. Despite being a little damp, we lingered, mesmerised by the falling water. Then, it was time to drive on.

We knew we wanted to visit Iceland's famous hot springs and decided that, given our schedule, going at night, after a long day in the car, would be the ideal time. When we arrived at Hvammsvík Hot Springs, the inky black already surrounded us. We'd chosen Hvammsvík because these are natural hot springs — there are eight, all with different temperatures. During the day, we'd seen relatively few people, but the hot springs were busy. Some visitors lived nearby, others were tourists like us. Many tourists had come with a guide.

Coming out of the changing room into the night sky was a shock — it was cold! Some people had brought water shoes — when our feet first hit the icy paths, we wished we'd thought of them too. Sinking into the first hot pool was a welcome relief. We spent a couple of hours going from pool to pool. Sitting in the pools we could hear the ocean and feel the icy salty breeze — and the soft conversation of other guests enjoying the hot natural springs.

<PullQuote variant="display">There, eerily dancing in the distance, were the northern lights!</PullQuote>

Later, walking back to the car, we turned to look back at the pools and saw so much more. There, eerily dancing in the distance, were the northern lights! We'd been told that looking at the lights through our cell phone would bring them even more alive — and for us that was true. Our own private tour of the northern lights was nothing short of amazing. We were just standing in a parking lot — watching one of the most amazing natural light shows in the world.

For our second night, we stayed in downtown Reykjavík. The reception at the hotel ([The Reykjavík EDITION](/go/marriott-reykjavik-edition)) was warm and friendly and our room was a delight — comfortable with that Scandinavian flair that you see all over Iceland. We'd chosen this location deliberately as we knew we were going north for the hot springs, but wanted to continue our tour of the Golden Circle in the morning with a visit to the Kerið crater.

That evening we took a short stroll around the restaurants and shops near our hotel. It was almost Christmas and the area was sparkling with lights. The restaurants looked cosy and we were tempted, but we were also tired so opted for one of Iceland's famous sausages at [Bæjarins Beztu pylsur](/go/bbp). The stand, which started in 1937, is an institution in Iceland.

The next morning, we went a little further, stopping at the massive Hallgrímskirkja before continuing to Rainbow Street. We didn't stay long. It was too early for the shops to be open and we had more to see before we headed to the airport. Our first stop was the Geysir geothermal centre — a stop along the famous Golden Circle. As we got out of the car we breathed in the tangy smell of sulphur. Then it was off to see the Kerið crater. The crater is known for the azure blue lake that sits at its centre. It was a cold, blustery day, but the lake looked beautiful. We walked around the rim, and then down the steps to its shores where we saw someone dipping his hands in. Based on his expression we figured it was icy cold.

From there it was time to head to the airport. On our way we drove past the volcanic embers that we'd seen before. This time though, it was daylight and they were more muted. We'd driven about 700 kilometres over the two days, but it didn't feel as though it was too much. We'd been in and out of the car throughout to explore, topped off with a long soak in the pools at Hvammsvík.

We left Iceland with the feeling that our jam packed visit was everything we'd wanted it to be. We also left knowing that we would be back. Our next visit would be at the opposite time of the year — the summer solstice. We knew there would be more people then, a lot more, but we also know there will be more daylight, a lot more. We have more exploring to do and will take advantage of every minute of it.

## The Essentials

**When to go**
December can be a great time to visit if you don't mind the cold. Crowds are small and days are short — just four hours of daylight around the Winter Solstice. But if you plan around the light, that's not a problem, it's part of the experience. Shoulder seasons — October and April — offer more daylight with fewer visitors than summer. Summer is optimal for the midnight sun and maximum daylight, but crowds are at their peak.

**Getting around**
Iceland is an easy self-drive country. The Ring Road is well-maintained. Keep in mind that roads in December can be icy — check conditions at [road.is](/go/road-is) before setting out. Guided tours of the Golden Circle are widely available if you'd prefer not to drive. We recommend lugged sole boots or crampons if you're planning to do any hiking.

## If You Have Time

**The Blue Lagoon**
Our schedule only left time for one hot springs, and we chose the natural pools at Hvammsvík. If you have time for a second, albeit very different, consider the Blue Lagoon. The manmade pools are instantly recognisable for their striking milky-blue, silica-rich water. It's located just 20 minutes from Keflavík Airport.

**Þingvellir National Park**
We passed through Þingvellir on our way to Geysir and Gullfoss but didn't stop. It's the first of the three classic Golden Circle sites, and worth building in real time for — a UNESCO World Heritage Site sitting directly on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, where the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates are visibly pulling apart. For something completely different from either hot springs, Silfra — the fissure between the plates — offers snorkelling in glacial water so clear it's often called the clearest in the world.
