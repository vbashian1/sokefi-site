Travel has always been what makes me feel alive. Perhaps it's because I had parents who were always on the move — always ready to pack their bags for the next adventure. As a young adult, with few plans in place other than a Eurail pass, some traveller's cheques, and a sleeping bag, I backpacked across Europe. I spent the summer wandering through small villages and big cities, usually arriving with little idea of what I wanted to see, but instead letting the location guide me. I sat beside mountain streams in the Alps and on beaches in France and Spain, trying new foods, seeing new things, and always — chatting with new people. Sometimes, I'd meet another backpacker going in the same direction and we'd join up for a few days. Some of those meetups were fleeting, others have become lifelong friends. I returned the next summer and the summer after that. I too, I discovered, shared the travel bug with my parents.

I continued my solo adventures into my 20s, thinking nothing of jumping on a plane and heading to a new place for a weekend. It was always sort of funny to see the reactions of my friends at work when they asked what I'd done for the weekend, and I said, I went to Paris or to Boston, or wherever it may have been. For me, a 9-5 work schedule wasn't a barrier to travel. It just meant that I needed to be a better planner and be ready to go when an opportunity presented itself.

<PullQuote variant="display">I don't wait for the perfect time to go — I just go — the same way I always have.</PullQuote>

Later, when I got married, my new husband quickly learned that being part of a couple wasn't going to curb my wanderlust. Fortunately, he too, enjoyed seeing new places and came along willingly. Still, even then, solo travel offered a sense of freedom — an open road ahead and nobody to tell me how fast — or how slow — to drive.

Things got a little more complicated when children came along, but like my parents, I quickly learned how to adapt, and our children's passports rapidly filled with new stamps as we continued to crisscross the world whenever we could. Now, with grown children, we continue to travel as a family, regularly meeting up in hub cities like London, Frankfurt, and New York before flying off to a new place. Time constraints and challenging schedules mean that some of those trips are now very short — we've become experts at the long-haul long weekend — other times, they're lazy road trips, a reminder of times past.

Midlife has also reopened the door to solo travel. My husband and I still love to travel as a couple, but with a job that has more freedom than his, I have been rejoicing in that sense of freedom I had in my backpacking years — this time though, with a little more luxury.

And I'm not alone. That's probably something that's surprised me most about my solo travel. As a young backpacker, I don't think it occurred to me that I was a solo traveller — I just went. But now, as part of a couple, and as a mother, being a solo traveller is different. In some ways, it's even more exciting to know that deep inside, I'm still me — the girl who set off to see the world. The freedom of solo travel still resonates — the knowing that if I get immersed in a museum, I can stay as long as I want, and the flip side, that I can also leave after 5 minutes without disappointing anyone. Yes, solo travel has its selfish side, but after years of putting others first, aren't we all ready to put ourselves first, at least once in a while?

<PullQuote>Solo travel offered a sense of freedom — an open road ahead and nobody to tell me how fast — or how slow — to drive.</PullQuote>

I like to think of myself not as a solo traveller, and instead as a modern nomad. Modern in that I'm not bound by norms and conventions of the past that might have implied that a woman travelling alone was doing so only because she wasn't in a romantic relationship or didn't have any friends. Modern in that I take full advantage of technology as I travel, using it to help with planning, on location, and to stay connected with my spouse, family, and friends. Modern in that I'm comfortable being on my own, and modern in that I don't wait for the perfect time to go — I just go — the same way I always have.

Solo travel for me is like the feeling you get when you step into a convertible and go whizzing off down the road, hair blowing in the wind — a little bit scary, but so much fun. If you're a lifelong solo traveller like me, you'll know that feeling of anticipation, that feeling of being fully alive. If you're new to solo travel, get in and come along. It's a great ride.
