Ever since I watched the cult classic Fast Times at Ridgemont High as a college student, I've dreamed of being my favourite character, Jeff Spicoli, and living the life of a surfer in Southern California. In my mind, Jeff was the ultimate Southern California surfer, carefree and dedicated to a life of tasty waves and a cool buzz. What more could you want? Never mind that the only waves he surfs in the movie are in his mind.

So, when a few years later — OK a few decades later — the opportunity came up to spend two weeks in Southern California, I knew exactly what I wanted to do. Live the life of Jeff Spicoli — or at least the life I imagined that he might live. Fortunately my wife was on board so away we went. Arriving into LAX, we quickly picked up a rental car — a nondescript SUV with no surfer vibes — and headed south. Our destination was Dana Point, a small beach town about halfway between Los Angeles and San Diego.

<PullQuote variant="display">The only problem? I didn't actually know how to surf and I didn't have a board.</PullQuote>

Dana Point is a known surf spot, a perfect place we thought, to spend our two weeks immersed in the local culture. Among surfers, Dana Point is known for Killer Dana, a wave break that once brought surfers from around the world. Today, Killer Dana no longer exists. The construction of a sea wall and harbour in the late 1960s changed the wave pattern. The town is still known for its surfing though, and claims Hobie Alter, maker of Hobie surfboards, as one of its former residents. For us though, it was the town's proximity to other famous surfing spots that was the real attraction. A few miles to the south is Trestles, a beach that was featured in the famous surf movie The Endless Summer and the location where the surfing competition will take place in the 2028 Olympic Games. A few miles to the north is Newport Beach, another surf spot favoured by those looking for some big waves.

Our first stop of course was the beach. We spent the afternoon watching neoprene-clad surfers gracefully conquer waves, sliding down them effortlessly, sometimes adding extra flourishes to extend their rides or make a switchback before they continued on. Looking out at them, I knew I was ready. I could already see myself skimming across the tops of the waves before sliding into the water to take another run. The only problem? I didn't actually know how to surf and I didn't have a board.

A few minutes later though, my wife sang out "problem solved". She'd booked me into a surf lesson the next morning. I couldn't wait.

The next day, fortified with a breakfast burrito — a wonderful brick-sized concoction of eggs, meat, and potatoes all wrapped up in a tortilla — we set off for Laguna Beach, the town immediately to the north. As we drove along the Pacific Coast Highway — or just Coast Highway as it's known locally — we exclaimed over lovely Laguna Beach. It's a town that we know we will return to. Today though, our mission was clear and so we headed for [La Vida Laguna](/go/viator-laguna-surf), the surf shop where I'd have my lesson.

<PullQuote>I picked up my board and headed out to the beach.<br />Jeff Spicoli, here I come.</PullQuote>

As I struggled into my own neoprene suit I thought about the surfers I'd seen the day before, easily changing in and out of their wetsuits on the beach and by their cars. I concluded it must be something they're born with. Me? Not quite the natural they were. As I do with any active trip like this, I took the extra step of protecting myself.

Finally ready, I picked up my board and headed out to the beach. Jeff Spicoli, here I come. It was a group class and our instructor was patient, initially showing us some moves on land before instructing us to paddle out. The moment had come! As I entered the water I gasped. It was cold even with the wetsuit. I quickly adapted though and spent the next hour paddling out and trying — without success — to catch a wave. How did the surfers I'd seen yesterday make it look so easy? Then, it happened. I finally caught a wave. There I was gliding across the surface of the water — it was a sense of exhilaration I knew I'd never forget. As I crashed down into the water, the ride over, I looked across and saw my wife giving me the thumbs up. She'd videoed the whole thing. My life as a surfer was forever immortalised in the tiny pixels of our 360 camera. It validated my existence as Jeff Spicoli surfer extraordinaire.

Over the course of our two weeks, I returned to the surf shop for some more lessons, eventually managing to maintain my ride for what seemed like a completely respectable 20 seconds at least. I was living — in my mind at least — the life of a surfer in Southern California.

Our water adventures didn't end there though. Back in Dana Point, my wife and I learned to paddleboard, taking a lesson we booked through [Viator](/go/viator-laguna). We started at Baby Beach, paddling around the harbour along with all the locals who start their day with an early morning paddle. We also returned to Laguna Beach to take a kayak tour along the coast, and then up to Newport for a full-day fishing trip where we caught what we figured would later turn up in one of the many fish tacos we consumed during the two weeks.

<Inset side="right" pick={1} focus="74% 77%" zoom={1.27} />

Fish tacos are one of those things that seem so completely Southern California to us. They're available just about everywhere and we tried many versions. Our favourite was a soft tortilla wrapped around mild grilled white fish, a slice of avocado, some pico de gallo, and a little cabbage.

The two weeks flew by. Our active water adventures in the morning were replaced with lazy afternoons at the beach or cruising along Coast Highway listening to the Beach Boys singing about the life we were seeing. While our nondescript SUV didn't quite give off the surf vibe that the real surfers so casually displayed, cool old cars with surfboards slung on their roofs or sticking out from the back window, we were there living our best lives.

<Gallery pick={[3]} />

We explored hidden coves and cool rock formations in Laguna Beach, took a hike at Crystal Cove, and participated in what we learned was another great part of Southern California, Cars and Coffee. We knew about the love affair that Californians have with their cars — we'd seen it on numerous past visits to the Golden State. This time though we went. Cars and Coffee is a loose term used to describe the gatherings of car enthusiasts on Saturday mornings. The gatherings take place all over Southern California in big parking lots and small ones. People bring their cars, often lovingly restored sports cars from the past, or unique camper vans, to share with anyone who wants to see them. We went to one of the largest gatherings, South OC Cars and Coffee in San Clemente. This meet-up takes place in a huge shopping mall parking lot and attracts hundreds of people — some visitors and some car owners — the cars and vans are parked in rows allowing people to wander through and admire. On our visit we spotted a beautifully restored Jaguar E-Type, a Shelby Cobra, and a DB5, the car that's often associated with James Bond. We saw rows of Aston Martins and Lamborghinis, muscle cars, and Woodies. There were VW buses and Unimogs. Like everyone else, we chatted with owners, looked enviously at the cars of our dreams, sat — at the invitation of the owners — in a couple and dreamed of driving one instead of our nondescript SUV.

<Inset side="left" pick={2} />

And then, all too soon, it was time to leave. Our two weeks of living like the locals — rising early to go to the water, spending time on the beach, eating fish tacos and breakfast burritos, enjoying California car culture — was over. We hadn't done any sightseeing. We hadn't ventured more than a few miles at a time. We hadn't done any of the usual things we do when we travel. We'd just lived life as locals — tasty waves and a cool buzz. It was a great trip and one we won't soon forget, especially now that my life as a surfer à la Jeff Spicoli is documented in a frame sitting on my desk.

## The Essentials
**Getting around**
Pick up a rental car at LAX — [Enterprise](/go/enterprise-lax) has a counter at the airport.

**On the water**
**Kayaking:** we took the tour out of Laguna Beach with [Virgin Experiences](/go/virgin-experience-kayak). [Viator](/go/viator-dana-point) also has options in Dana Point and Newport Beach, where you can rent paddleboards too.

**Fishing:** we've fished out of both Dana Point and Newport Beach, booked through [Viator](/go/viator-dana-point). Read the reviews there to pick the trip that suits you best.

**Where to stay**
**A room with a view:** our favourites are [The Ritz-Carlton, Laguna Niguel](/go/marriott-ritz-carlton-laguna-niguel), in Dana Point — perched on a bluff over Salt Creek Beach. Or the [Laguna Cliffs Marriott Resort & Spa](/go/marriott-laguna-cliffs), also in Dana Point, currently mid-renovation — some rooms have a view, others don't, so worth checking when you book.

**A room with a kitchen:** [Marriott's Newport Coast Villas](/go/marriott-newport-coast-villas), near Crystal Cove State Beach.

**For Beach Boys fans:** [Riviera Beach & Shores, a Hilton Vacation Club](/go/hilton-riviera-beach), in Capistrano Beach at the southern end of Dana Point. It's located across the highway from the beach, but it's the beach immortalised by the Beach Boys when they sang about Doheny Way.
