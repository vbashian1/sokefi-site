I was once asked "Do you cruise?" and before I could answer the person asking the question answered it herself: "We cruise." At the time, I could honestly say that not only had I no idea what she was talking about — to me it sounded more like some sort of debauchery — but also that once I'd worked out that she was talking about taking a trip on a boat — and not just any boat, but a huge cruise ship, that the answer was a definitive and resounding no! In my mind, cruising was something old people — I was in my mid-30s at the time — or those who were not at all adventurous might consider doing. After all, the very nature of a cruise is that most decisions are made for you once you've identified whether you want to book an inside cabin or spring for something with a window or better yet, a balcony. Beyond that, you simply need to show up and then decide whether you want to partake in any of the on-board activities or shore excursions. This was not my style, and the "Do you cruise?" phrase became a catch-all in conversations with friends to describe something we'd never do. Until now that is. Sure, we're all older — and possibly fall into the age group that was in my mind when I thought about who took cruises, but these days, cruising has lost its past retiree identity and is cruising into new waters.

Modern cruising, with its vast array of destinations, trip lengths, and ship types, offers something for just about everyone. You can still cruise around the Caribbean of course, or take that Disney cruise if you're into that sort of trip, but now there are cruises to tempt even the most anti-cruiser among us. For many, the dream cruise destination is still the Northwest Passage around Alaska and across Canada. Breathtaking photos of icebergs and whales splashing down into icy blue waters make it clear why Alaska and the entire Northwest Passage remain a standout option in North America. On the other side of the Atlantic there is strong demand for cruises into the equally cold waters of Norway and up to the Arctic Circle. As with Alaskan cruises, Norwegian fjord and Arctic Circle cruises also offer the chance to see — and if you're there at the right time — hear — icebergs looming above the water, polar bears, and whales. Those preferring warmer destinations can choose from an array of options including sailing around the Canary Islands, along the coast of the Riviera, French Polynesia, and so much more.

If you have time, long-haul cruises offer a chance to really acquire your sea legs. If you've ever wanted to recapture the glamour of crossing the Atlantic by ship, consider the Queen Mary 2 transatlantic trip. These seven-day sailings offer a slower, more immersive experience. Those who've completed this journey will tell you that it's not a cruise, instead it's a crossing that comes complete with experiences unlike anything else at sea. I did the Literature Festival at Sea, a fantastic week of author presentations and talks, book signings, and more. If theatre or music are more your style, book the London Theatre at Sea week or the classical music crossing. If you're into cooking, you might like one of the special culinary/chef crossings. You choose which side of the Atlantic to start on, and fly one way and go by ship the other.

Riverboat cruising has also become a popular way to slow down and enjoy the world around you. I recently spent a very enjoyable few days on a micro cruise, drifting along the Rhine, occasionally getting off the boat to spend a few hours wandering around one of the many small towns that line its banks, before returning to the boat later in the afternoon. Consider a cruise on the Danube and enjoy some of the vineyards along the way, or explore the châteaux along the Loire. Choose a summer cruise to enjoy Europe without the crowds and the heat. Choose a winter cruise to enjoy the magic of Europe's Christmas markets.

You can now even see some of the furthest points in the world from the rails of a ship. Antarctica, long a destination for small, usually more utilitarian, educational sailings, has become a destination for luxury liners. As you'd expect, the bigger ships don't have the ability to offer the "up close and in person" experience that a small ship can, but they do offer the chance to see the magnificence of Antarctica from very comfortable surroundings. One of the newest additions to this hyper-luxury cruise market is journeys to the North Pole. Only one company currently offers these trips — the French company [Ponant](/go/ponant), aboard its icebreaker Le Commandant Charcot. The sailings take about two weeks, during which the only thing to see is often water, and there are no port calls, but as they say, the journey is part of the destination and when you get there, you'll know that you've gone where few others have.

I've also been recommending cruises to friends who are just beginning their journeys as fellow solo travellers. For new solo travellers, cruises can be a great way to experience solo travel for the first time and gain the confidence to take other solo trips. Once you're on board, it's up to you whether you engage in shipside yoga classes, visit the spa, take a line dancing class, learn to cook, or participate in any of the many activities that are usually available. You could choose to do none of these and just spend the time as I have been known to do, reading a book and watching the water, a total escape from the craziness of everyday life.

So far, my preference has been for small-ship adventures. I've found that the smaller ship format is ideal for solo travellers for many reasons. At the top of the list, of course, is what it advertises — a smaller ship that has a smaller number of passengers. In practical terms this means that you're more likely to run into the same people over the course of the journey and develop a fun on-board camaraderie. Meeting new people and learning about their adventures has always been one of the great joys of solo travel and it's something that small-ship sailings facilitate. Choosing a small-ship adventure also often means a more varied itinerary — stops where larger ships that cater to passengers seeking more traditional cruise experiences might skip. I've found that some of these excursions — quick looks at something new — often become places that I return to for longer trips. Smaller ships also often offer more flexible itineraries and pricing schemes, something that as a solo traveller I appreciate. This is starting to change though and several of the larger cruise lines now offer solo travel pricing that is in line with more traditional couples pricing.

Some of the newest trends in cruising are the micro cruise — short 2–4-day sailings that often leave from nearby ports and then stick close to the local shoreline, and the adults-only cruise. Both can be optimal for solo travellers. Adults-only cruises are, by definition, designed for the 18+ traveller. [Virgin Voyages](/go/gotosea-virgin-voyages) is one of the newest players in this space, offering adults-only cruises on full-size luxury ships. Virgin offers Solo Sailor events where solo travellers can meet other solo travellers, helping to eliminate the angst that may come with solo travelling.
