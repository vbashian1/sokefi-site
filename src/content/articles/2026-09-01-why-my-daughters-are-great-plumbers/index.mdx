"Pass me the pointy things," my daughter said to me. "You mean the needle-nosed pliers?" I asked. "Whatever" (read some attitude into this), she replied. "Just pass them to me."

We were fixing a shower together — a, to me, somewhat complicated process given that it involves both plumbing and electricity. It's an instant hot water shower for what amounts to a basic cabin in the woods that has limited traditional plumbing and electrical services — my wife likes to call it camping, but I say it's a step above. We were there for a much-anticipated week with our adult kids. The shower had abruptly broken and thanks to the foresight of my wife — and the fact that we'd been in a similar situation before and spent a very smelly week without showers — we had a backup at the ready. So, when I heard the shouts this time that the shower wasn't working, I put on my metaphorical Superman cape and sprang into action — no problem, we had backups ready. I quickly moved to remove the cover and then stopped and stared at the tangle of wires and pipes in front of me. I'm not what you'd call handy — although being a man, I'm supposed to be, at least in society's view — and so I started to grab tools and bang away, sure that with enough brute strength I could get this done.

Fortunately, at that point my daughter wandered by. She took a glance and said, "Dad, you're doing that all wrong." "What do you mean?" I asked. "What suddenly makes you an expert on showers?" She said, "Well, you've clearly got to turn off the power before you start disconnecting electrical wires." Oops — in my bull-in-a-china-shop haste, I'd forgotten that tiny, yet quite vital point. "Done," I announced, returning a few minutes later. By that time she'd read through the instruction manual — something I hadn't bothered to do given that it was written in 4-point type and I couldn't even see which page of what seemed to be a 72-page document was in English. She then said, "According to this, we need to disconnect the water pipe first." I heard the "we" and thought — hmm, this could be good.

So side by side, we tackled the project — my daughter, with her more slender fingers, clearly better suited to the tiny electrical connections than mine, leading the charge. I became her assistant.

Later, we joked about it all — and I, of course, was the subject of various other, shall we say, reminiscences by four adult children. Clearly they found much more mirth in what had been, over the years, frustrating and stressful efforts to fix broken sump pumps and the regular floods that came with them, broken dishwashers, leaky car tyres — the list goes on. Now, as adults themselves, they were dealing with some of those same problems. My sons, it seems, share my same take-charge attitude — I bluster my way through things even when I have no clue what I'm doing. In contrast, my daughters take a completely different approach — just as my daughter had earlier that day — patiently reading through instructions before leaping into action, thinking logically about how to approach tasks, which seems to save more time in the long run than my approach of figuring out how to fix things on the backside.

It made me realise that as a so-called girl dad — although my girls are now young women with successful careers, ironically both in traditionally men's professions — my daughters have much to teach me. How to fix showers, for example, but also about being a dad. And I started seeing them through new eyes — they're now independent women around the same age that my wife was when we first met — and just as my wife and I tackled unfamiliar tasks in our first home together, side by side, both of us equally unsure of what we were doing, I realised that my role as a dad is changing. My daughters deserve the same respect my wife did. It's not that they're exceptional plumbers or electricians — more the simple recognition that they might know as much, or more, about something than I do, or in the case of the shower, at least have the patience to figure things out.

And that's my take on why my daughters are great plumbers.
