We go deep into the Mississippi Delta to drive Highway 61 — the Blues Highway.

## The Delta heart

We turned off the main highway — Highway 61 — onto a dirt road, leaving a cloud of dust in our wake. Fields stretched out for miles on one side and a swampy stream filled with trees and underbrush was on the other. A few minutes later we spotted our destination, a derelict wooden shack with what looked to be an outhouse standing in the back. In front was the now-familiar blue plaque. We turned off the car — it was then that we heard the buzz. The natural sounds of the Mississippi Delta filled our ears; the locusts were singing, loud and unapologetically, their din a reminder that at one time people drove out on this road to hear a different type of music — the music that was born in the Delta. The Mississippi Blues. The music that provided the foundation for rock 'n' roll.

We were at Po' Monkey's, one of the longest-surviving juke joints along Mississippi's Highway 61, or the Blues Highway as it's also known. In its heyday the small building was a regular destination for locals. Later it became a place where blues musicians and fans from around the world would gather. It closed in 2016 following the death of its owner, Willie "Po' Monkey" Seaberry, a Delta juke-joint legend. Today its storied past is memorialised on the blue plaque that stands in the weeds that now surround the building.

<Gallery pick={[0]} />

The plaque is part of the [Mississippi Blues Trail](/go/ms-blues-trail), an effort by the Mississippi Blues Commission to preserve the places where blues music was born and tell the stories of the musicians who created it. You'll find the plaques all through the Delta — along Highways 61 and 49, and in the small towns that make up the region. They provide a roadmap of sorts to exploring the area. You'll find them outside dilapidated buildings like Po' Monkey's, in front of still-operating businesses like the Blue Front Café, in seemingly random fields marking an individual's birthplace, and — one of our favourites — across from the Mississippi State Penitentiary, immortalised by former inmate and blues musician Bukka White in his song "Parchman Farm."

<Gallery pick={[1]} />

To get a sense of just how important the Delta blues were to rock 'n' roll, you only need to see the telegram from the members of the Rolling Stones to the great Muddy Waters. It's on display in the Delta Blues Museum in Clarksdale. The telegram, sent in 1975, says that without Muddy the band would not be what it was. The band, of course, took its name from a Muddy Waters song, and went on to record several of his songs and those of other Delta blues musicians.

In this sense, a trip through the Mississippi Delta is a pilgrimage. Seeing where the blues was born gives it a deeper meaning — some go as far as saying it's a spiritual journey.

The Delta is vast, and isolated. It's a place where, for many, times were — and still are — tough. You hear the struggle in the rawness of the music itself, and you see it in the small towns where the good days have come and gone. Yet as you drive through those small towns, you feel the warmth of the people who continue to live there. The old chairs sitting outside nearly every home provide a sense of community. They're where people sit and watch the world go by, waving at cars as they pass. It's a place where time has seemingly stood still. It's a place to savour and enjoy — adopting the local, unhurried pace of life, where questions become conversations and stories.

We stopped into a tiny guitar shop in Clarksdale and ended up spending an hour talking with the proprietor. He told us of well-known musicians and celebrities who'd passed through his store, showing us photos — some recent, some a little older. He told us of his younger days, when he worked in the fields before becoming a banker. It was entirely unexpected and wonderful. We only left when another couple came in and asked if he remembered them. They'd been there the year before.

We asked for directions to Muddy Waters' birthplace from a trio of men sitting in the shade outside a small business in Rolling Fork. As so many others had, they asked where we were from — and the conversation went from there. When we finally got around to getting the directions we'd stopped for, we realised we'd been talking for half an hour. We found it especially funny because a man we'd met in Vicksburg had told us he was originally from Rolling Fork. He'd told us the pace was slower there.

A stop for lunch in Indianola at Betty's Place, a local barbecue joint, invited more conversation — and a chance to sample a Muddy Water, a local take on the more widely known Arnold Palmer: a sweet mix of lemonade and iced tea. We listened to the buzz of conversation around us. A 94-year-old had driven over — it was his birthday. A policeman wandered in to pick up a takeout order. The man working behind the counter knew everyone and greeted them like family. We were the only out-of-towners there, but we knew we weren't the first: the walls were covered with the signatures and messages of people from around the world who'd also stopped into the tiny restaurant as they drove Highway 61.

When we stopped at the Blue Front Café in Bentonia — the oldest juke joint in the state that's still operational — it was closed. Someone at the building next door wandered over and greeted us. He said the owner, legendary bluesman Jimmy "Duck" Holmes, had gone to the eye doctor but would be back before too long. He invited us to wait. We did — and about an hour and a half later, Jimmy returned. We spent the time like the locals do: in slow conversation, waving at passers-by, and simply enjoying the peace of a steamy day in August in the Mississippi Delta.

## Planning your own Blues Highway drive

While the nickname suggests a straight line with stops along the way, the reality is that visiting the stops on the Blues Highway means heading off the main road. The beauty of the journey, though, is that following the plaques takes you through towns you might otherwise simply drive by. Driving out of Rolling Fork, we happened upon Nitta Yuma, population 20 — once a thriving community, today a scattering of small cottages, a post office, a church, and a main store. The buildings provide a snapshot of the past, and inspiration for the independent filmmakers who visit the area.

Exploring the Blues Highway is personal. Some people drive through in a single day, stopping at only a few plaques; others spend a week or more. While Highway 61 technically starts in New Orleans and runs some 1,400 miles north into Minnesota, our focus was Mississippi, almost entirely within the Delta.

**Where to base yourself.** If you're starting at one end with the goal of ending at the other, Jackson makes a good midway point — we stayed at [The Westin Jackson](/go/marriott-westin-jackson) downtown; look for the case of guitars owned by famous blues musicians in the lobby. Bentonia, and the Blue Front Café, is about a 45-minute drive away.

Better yet, treat **Cleveland** as a centre point — somewhere to stay for a few days and drive out to different spots along the trail. We stayed at [The Cotton House](/go/marriott-cotton-house-cleveland), a boutique hotel in the centre of town on the former site of a general store. Independent shops line the main street, the Delta State University campus is close by — along with the **[GRAMMY Museum Mississippi](/go/grammy-museum-ms)**, one of only two GRAMMY Museums in the country. It's a 45-minute drive to Clarksdale, where you can hear live blues most nights at Red's.

### A day in Clarksdale

Start at the **[Delta Blues Museum](/go/delta-blues-museum)**, a museum entirely dedicated to preserving the history of the blues, before heading to the **Crossroads** — the famous junction where Highways 61 and 49 intersect. It was here that bluesman Robert Johnson is said to have sold his soul to the devil, although some say that fellow musician Tommy Johnson made the deal first. If you have time, drop into **Bluestown Music** (317 Delta Avenue); bring cash if you're planning to buy, as they don't take plastic. Before you leave, stop into **Red's** for some live blues. If you're there in April, catch the **[Juke Joint Festival](/go/juke-joint-festival)**; in August, the **[Sunflower River Blues & Gospel Festival](/go/sunflower-fest)**.

### Out from Cleveland — Stovall & Dockery

A visit to the blue plaque at **Stovall Plantation** gives a sense of the sheer vastness of the Delta's fields. Today it's soybeans; when Muddy Waters lived and worked here, it was cotton. It's the place where, in 1941, Alan Lomax first recorded Muddy. You can see the cabin's main room in the Delta Blues Museum.

<Gallery pick={[3]} />

From there, head to **Dockery Farms**, often called one of the cradles of the blues — the home ground of Charley Patton. You'll see a well-preserved service station emblazoned with Coca-Cola signs; behind it, a more dilapidated building houses a cotton gin. You can walk inside to see the machinery and imagine what it was like to work there.

### Indianola — B.B. King's hometown

About half an hour from Cleveland, Indianola is the hometown of B.B. King. The **[B.B. King Museum and Delta Interpretive Center](/go/bb-king-museum)** tells his story and is also his final resting place. Have lunch at **Betty's Place**, the local barbecue joint where B.B. King used to eat. Before you leave, sign the wall.

### A day in Vicksburg

If you have time, spend a day in **Vicksburg**, birthplace of **Willie Dixon** — the songwriter behind so much of the Chicago blues sound. His house is gone now, but you can see the little house beside the vacant lot where his once stood and imagine what it was like. Wander the main street: along with the independent shops, you'll find a Coca-Cola museum — the **Biedenharn Coca-Cola Museum** — where the first bottle of Coca-Cola was bottled, back in 1894. Stop in for a look at all sorts of Coca-Cola memorabilia.

### Rolling Fork — Muddy Waters' birthplace

An hour or so north of Vicksburg is the town of **Rolling Fork**, the place Muddy Waters always called home. He moved to Stovall Plantation as a small child but never forgot his birthplace. The town also lays claim to the teddy bear: the name traces to a 1902 hunt nearby, where Theodore Roosevelt refused to shoot a captured black bear, and carved bears still gather around the plaque that tells the story. A 2023 tornado left damage that's yet to be fully repaired, but the bears — and the sign recounting the tale — are still there.
